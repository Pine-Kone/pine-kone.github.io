The Formulas

=COUNT(B2:B50)
=COUNTA(B2:B50)
=SUM(B2:B50)
=AVERAGE(B2:B50)
=MAX(B2:B50)
=MIN(B2:B50)

The basics: counting and summarizing a range

COUNT tells you how many cells in a range hold actual numbers, while COUNTA counts any cell that isn't blank - numbers, text, whatever's there. SUM adds everything up, AVERAGE divides that total by how many values went into it, and MAX and MIN pull out the highest and lowest number in the range. These six show up constantly - they're usually the first thing anyone reaches for once a sheet has more than a handful of rows.

=STDEV.S(B2:B50)
=CONFIDENCE.NORM(0.05, STDEV.S(B2:B50), COUNT(B2:B50))
=FORECAST(D2, B2:B50, A2:A50)

Beyond the average: spread and prediction

STDEV.S measures how spread out a set of values is around the average - a small standard deviation means the numbers cluster tightly, a large one means they're scattered. CONFIDENCE.NORM uses that same spread to build a margin of error around the average (a 95% confidence interval here, since 0.05 is the significance level). FORECAST goes a step further and predicts a new value along a trend line, estimating what y should be for a new x given a set of known x/y pairs.

=COUNTIFS(C2:C50, "Complete", D2:D50, ">="&$D$1)
=SUMIFS(B2:B50, C2:C50, "Complete")
=AVERAGEIFS(B2:B50, C2:C50, "Complete")

Aggregating with conditions - and anchoring a cell in place

COUNTIFS, SUMIFS, and AVERAGEIFS are the conditional versions of COUNT, SUM, and AVERAGE - each one only counts, adds, or averages the rows that match one or more criteria (here, rows marked "Complete"). Notice the dollar signs in $D$1: that's an anchored, or absolute, reference. Without them, dragging a formula down to the next row shifts every cell reference along with it; anchoring a cell with $ locks it in place so the formula keeps pointing at the same spot no matter where you copy it.

=IF(AND(B2>=70, C2="Passed"), "Eligible", "Not Eligible")
=IF(OR(B2<50, C2="Failed"), "Review", "OK")
=NOT(C2="Failed")

Making decisions with boolean logic

IF is the workhorse of spreadsheet logic: test a condition, return one value if it's true and another if it's false. AND and OR let you test more than one condition at once - AND needs every condition to be true, OR just needs one. NOT simply flips a TRUE or FALSE result to its opposite. Combined, these let a single cell make a real decision instead of just displaying a number.

=VLOOKUP(A2, PlotTypes!$A$2:$C$10, 3, FALSE)
=HLOOKUP(A2, RateTable!$B$1:$F$3, 2, FALSE)

Looking values up in another table

VLOOKUP searches down the first column of a table for a match, then returns a value from a column you specify to the right of it - useful any time you need to pull a related value (a rate, a name, a category) from a reference table instead of typing it in by hand. HLOOKUP does the same thing sideways, searching across the first row of a table instead of down the first column. The FALSE at the end of both tells Excel to look for an exact match only, rather than the closest one.

=LEN(A2)
=SEARCH("@", A2)
=LEFT(A2, 5)
=MID(A2, 3, 4)
=RIGHT(A2, 4)

Pulling text apart

LEN tells you how many characters are in a cell - handy for validating input length. SEARCH finds the position of one piece of text inside another (here, where the @ sign falls in an email address). LEFT, MID, and RIGHT then slice a piece of text out from the start, the middle, or the end of a string, based on a starting position and a length you give it. Together they let you break a single text cell - a name, an address, a code - into its individual pieces.

=HOUR(B2)
=MINUTE(B2)
=SECOND(B2)
=HOUR(B2)&":"&MINUTE(B2)&":"&SECOND(B2)&" - "&C2

Breaking down and rebuilding timestamps

HOUR, MINUTE, and SECOND each pull one piece out of a time value. Since Excel stores dates and times as plain numbers behind the scenes, prying out just the hour or the minute takes a dedicated function rather than simple math. The last line puts them back together with the & operator, concatenating the hour, minute, and second into a single readable timestamp - and tacking on a text label from another cell besides, the same trick used any time you need to combine numbers and text into one string.

=RATE(B2, C2, D2)
=EFFECT(B3, C3)
=FV(B4, C4, D4, E4)
=NPER(B5, C5, D5)

The payment functions

These four are built for loan and savings math. RATE solves for the interest rate on a loan or investment given its term, payment, and starting value. EFFECT converts a nominal (stated) annual interest rate into its effective rate once compounding is factored in - the number that actually reflects what you're paying or earning. FV calculates the future value of a series of payments, and NPER solves for the opposite: how many payment periods it'll take to reach a goal. Between the four, you can solve for whichever piece of a loan or savings plan you don't already know.
